# Belgium

Reviewed on 28 September 2026, mainly from official sources in French, with supporting English and Dutch pages. This is the third country review, after the [United Kingdom](united-kingdom.md) and [Austria](austria.md). It is a desk review: each topic from [the codebook](../codebook/codebook.md) was looked for in the federal Football Act and its royal decrees, Interior (BeSafe) pages, Federal Police / NFIP descriptions, and the Council of Europe country profile. Recommendation Rec(2022)2 was also searched for by name.

Belgium is a State Party to the Saint-Denis Convention. It signed on 29 November 2016, ratified on 9 September 2024, and the Convention entered into force for Belgium on 1 November 2024. That is after Rec(2022)2 was adopted. No source checked in this review cites Recommendation Rec(2022)2 by name. The ratification notice on BeSafe names the DG Sécurité & Prévention of the SPF Intérieur, the treaty service of SPF Affaires étrangères, and the Bureau DAO Sport of the Belgian Federal Police.

## The short answer

Belgium already has, and has had since 1998–1999, the closest national match among the three country files so far to both annexes of Rec(2022)2 for **football**: a dedicated federal Football Act (Annex 1 style) and a dedicated royal decree on steward engagement (Annex 2 style). Organiser duties, a mandated safety officer, stewards, local multi-agency conventions, stadium safety norms, CCTV, ticketing, administrative and judicial stadium bans with a central file, and a national football information point in the Federal Police are all required in law.

It has not adopted the recommendation as a new post-2022 model statute. The framework is football-scoped (national and international matches in designated divisions), not a single law for every sports event. Stewarding for football sits outside ordinary private-security licensing as a dedicated regime. Disability access and a Disability Access Officer are weaker in the statute than stewarding and bans: club and Pro League practice is visible; a hard Football Act duty was not opened.

Where this review could read the consolidated Football Act, the steward and coordination royal decrees, the 2023 amending Act, and Interior / CoE secondary sources, that is stated. Association licensing books were not opened end to end.

## What has changed since 1 September 2022

These are the changes found. The Football Act and core royal decrees themselves were already in place.

- **12 May 2022.** The action plan “Ensemble pour un Football sûr” was published (just before the cut-off). It remains the published multi-agency zero-tolerance plan on racism, illegal pyrotechnics, and physical violence. Implementation and reporting after September 2022 continue on BeSafe.
- **8 December 2022.** First national Football Steward Day (SPF Intérieur and URBSFA/RBFA): recognition of stewards, presentation of the action plan and a draft Football Act reform, and an e-learning training module.
- **2022.** National Chamber for Civil Exclusions (NKBU / Chambre nationale pour les exclusions civiles) reported as set up by the RBFA and Pro League for private-law stadium bans.
- **16 July 2023.** Loi du 19 juin 2023 entered into force. It strengthens organiser duties on pyrotechnics and anti-identification objects; allows stewards and mandated safety officers to check ticket–identity correspondence; raises administrative ban maxima to ten years; and sets higher minimum fines and ban lengths for violence against stewards and other safety staff, racism/xenophobia, and pyrotechnics. It also amends the private-security law so designated gardiennage agents may carry out the same ticket–identity check at football stadium entries.
- **9 September 2024 / 1 November 2024.** Ratification of the Saint-Denis Convention; entry into force for Belgium.
- **2025–July 2026.** Further Football Act reform announced and approved in first reading by the Council of Ministers (cabinet of Interior Minister Bernard Quintin): higher minimum fines, heavier racism and violence minima, immediate police stadium bans pending final decision, and a single SPF Intérieur database of administrative, judicial, and civil bans for ticket checks. When this review closed, that reform was not yet in force as law.

## Federal football law — one statute, many decrees

**Football Act.** The loi du 21 décembre 1998 relative à la sécurité lors des matches de football is the core statute. In the consolidated text read here it requires: organiser precautionary measures and an annual safety convention with emergency services and police (art. 5); a mandated safety officer (art. 6); stewards of both sexes (art. 7); a local advisory council where a club stages multiple national matches on the same pitch (art. 9); house rules with civil exclusion; spectator flow and segregation; ticket management; CCTV; and an internal emergency plan with evacuation, tested on a statutory cycle (art. 10). Title III–IV create sports-event administrative offences and stadium bans. Title V adds judicial stadium bans. Art. 24ter allows an administrative travel/exit ban linked to a stadium ban of two years or more. Art. 45 keeps a central ban file and photo file.

**Stewarding.** The arrêté royal du 25 mai 1999 is a dedicated stewarding regulation: selection (including certificat de bonnes conduite, vie et moeurs and no recent stadium ban), Interior-approved training (theory, practice including first aid and evacuation, five-match stage), annual refresh, hierarchy (steward / chef de division / chef steward), fluorescent STEWARD clothing, and a federation photo ID card. Volunteers and paid stewards fall under the same rules. Stewards have no police powers.

**Coordination and the Football Cell.** The arrêté royal du 15 juin 1999 sets safety-officer conditions and the local advisory council, and gives a regulatory base to the Cellule football (now the Football Safety Service in SPF Intérieur DG Sécurité & Prévention): phenomenon analysis, advice to the Minister, coordination forum, assistance to sanctioning officials, stadium-norm expertise, and international representation.

**Infrastructure and CCTV.** The arrêté royal du 6 juillet 2013 sets binding stadium safety norms. The arrêté royal du 22 février 2006 regulates CCTV. Mayor attestation and fire/stability expert reports feed compliance; the Football Cell inspects and handles derogations.

**National football information point.** The CoE country profile places the football coordination bureau of the Federal Police as NFIP. The head of the Federal Police Major Sports Event Department is described publicly as heading the Belgian NFIP.

**Supporter liaison.** The Football Act defines a supporters liaison officer (SLO). The CoE profile and club practice state that SLO appointment is mandatory for the top two national divisions (royal decree of 16 June 2018 as reported; the decree text itself was not re-opened line by line in this pass).

## Topic by topic

Each topic is one cluster from the codebook. The word in bold is the judgement for Belgium as a whole for football events in the Act’s scope. Gaps outside football, or resting only on association practice, are stated.

### National governance and coordination — met for the Football Cell and local councils; partial for strategy

The SPF Intérieur Football Safety Service / Cellule football coordinates national football safety policy and sanctions administration. The Federal Police NFIP covers police information exchange. Together with the CoE profile’s national committee reference, that meets the national coordinating-body duty for football. Local multi-agency coordination is required by art. 5 conventions and art. 9 local advisory councils (with SLO, emergency services, police, and steward command represented in practice).

Roles of public authorities, organiser, safety officer, and venue are stated across the Act and ARs. That is met.

A published action plan exists (Ensemble pour un Football sûr). It is not read here as a full national strategy covering safety, security, and service equally and kept under a formal review cycle of the Rec(2022)1 type. Partial for strategy.

### Venue safety duties — met for football in scope

The Act requires organiser safety measures inside and outside the practical match setting through conventions, house rules, stewards, and police interface. The Convention’s integrated language is now binding internationally for Belgium (in force 1 November 2024). A mandated safety officer and house rules as a condition of entry are clear. Met for designated football; not mapped for other sports.

### Safety certification and licensing — met

Organisers may use only stadia that meet royal safety norms (art. 4). The mayor’s annual attestation, fire and stability reports, SPF inspections, and art. 8bis capacity reduction give a public authority that can constrain or refuse continued use at the agreed safe capacity. Enforcement includes organiser fines and capacity cuts. Met for football stadia in scope; there is no separate independent stadium-safety agency of the English SGSA type.

### Venue infrastructure and operations — met for top football

The 2013 norms regulate spectator areas, routes, command arrangements, and related infrastructure. Segregation of rival supporters is a statutory organiser duty. CCTV is mandatory for the matches art. 10 §2 covers. Operational arrangements with police and emergency services sit in the art. 5 convention and match briefings.

### Emergency and contingency planning — met

Art. 10 §2 requires an internal emergency plan covering evacuation, tested annually for the first two years an organiser falls under the Act, then every three years with all partners. Steward training includes first aid and evacuation. Contingency for foreseeable disruption is covered through that plan and the multi-agency convention rather than a separately labelled contingency statute.

### Venue safety service — met

Stewards are mandatory. They work under the mandated safety officer. Volunteers are expressly allowed and limited by the same recruitment, training, and card rules as paid stewards.

### Access, tickets, and removal from the ground — met for tickets, search, and expulsion; partial for disability access

Ticketing is regulated by royal decree; from 2023 stewards may check identity against the ticket name and refuse entry on mismatch. Prohibited items and behaviour are set in house rules and Title III (including pyrotechnics and objects used to avoid identification). Same-sex superficial searches and refusal/expulsion rules are statutory. Disability access: club DAO pages and Pro League accessibility work are visible; a Football Act article requiring accessible facilities or a DAO post was not found. Partial for inclusion; met for the other access indicators.

### Policing, risk, and exclusion — met for bans, database, and NFIP; partial for statutory risk categories

Police roles are coordinated through conventions, circulars (including OOP 38 on spotters), and the NFIP. Administrative, judicial, civil, and interim security-measure stadium bans exist, with perimeter and travel/exit options. The central ban file is statutory. Risk-based policing and “groupes à risques” appear in practice and steward training; a formal statutory supporter-category scheme with published safeguards was not opened. Partial for risk categories; otherwise met.

### Service, dialogue, and inclusion — met for SLO, discrimination, and funded projects; partial for dialogue charter and DAO

SLO is a legal function for the top divisions. Discrimination and racist conduct are specifically addressed in the Act and were hardened in 2023. Interior-funded fan/prevention projects form part of the published approach. A national supporters’ charter as a statutory dialogue duty was not found. DAO is practice, not a confirmed statutory post.

### Sports-event offences — met

Violence, disorder, missile throwing, unlawful pitch/zone entry, hate/incitement, pyrotechnics, and related conduct are punishable under the Football Act (and general criminal law where applicable). Offences against stewards and other event safety staff carry higher minimum administrative sanctions since 2023. Met.

### Steward status and authorisation — met

Stewards are defined as the organiser’s safety staff, distinct from police and from ordinary private-security agents. Qualification via approved training and a federation ID card is required before acting. Vetting includes the good-conduct certificate and ban-history check. The dedicated regime authorises club engagement of stewards; commercial gardiennage is carved in only for specified ticket–identity checks.

### Steward duties and powers — met

Crowd management, entry and ticket control, search, refusal of entry, emergency first response, and pre-agreed cooperation with police are set out in the Act and the 1999 AR. Powers are expressly limited: no force, no police powers.

### Conduct and visibility — partial

Welcome and information duties are statutory. A free-standing written code of conduct covering courtesy, neutrality, and non-discrimination by name was not opened. Fluorescent STEWARD clothing and a photo ID card are required; a unique personal number on the garment was not found.

### Records and supervision — partial for handbook; met for records

Training and card records sit with the organiser and the federation; eligibility ties into the ban file. An issued written handbook of duties and emergency arrangements for every steward was not confirmed; pre-match briefings are mandatory.

### Training and exercises — met for initial training and exercises; not found for CT/discrimination modules

Initial training before deployment, annual refresh, and statutory emergency-plan exercises are clear. Counter-terrorism awareness and anti-discrimination as named mandatory syllabus items were not found in AR art. 10.

### Spectator service and private-security law — met

Stewards have a statutory welcome and information role. Training and deployment sit under the dedicated Football Act regime, which states how that regime relates to private-security law (exclusion of ordinary stewarding from gardiennage licensing; limited 2023 gateway for identity checks).

## What a Belgian reader would be asked

These are the points this review could not close. They are the survey, pre-filled from the findings above.

1. Is the Plan Ensemble pour un Football sûr treated as the national strategy on safety, security and service, and is it kept under formal periodic review?
2. Which instrument requires disability access at designated football stadia, and is a Disability Access Officer a licensing or statutory condition?
3. Is a Disability Access Officer required by law or only by Pro League / club practice?
4. Is there a binding written steward code of conduct covering courtesy, neutrality, and non-discrimination?
5. Is a unique steward number required on clothing, or only the fluorescent STEWARD garment and photo ID card?
6. Does the Interior-approved steward syllabus mandatorily cover counter-terrorism awareness and discriminatory behaviour?
7. Do stewards receive their duties and emergency arrangements in a written handbook?
8. Has any Belgian instrument adopted or cited Recommendation Rec(2022)2 by name since 2022?
9. Has the 2025–2026 reform consolidating administrative, judicial and civil stadium bans into a single SPF Intérieur database entered into force?
10. Beyond football, which instruments apply the integrated safety, security and service approach to other sports events?
