# United Kingdom

Reviewed on 23 September 2026, from official sources in English. This is the first country review. It is a desk review: each topic from [the codebook](../codebook/codebook.md) was looked for in UK legislation, regulators’ pages, and government guidance. The recommendation itself was also searched for by name.

The United Kingdom is a State Party to the Saint-Denis Convention. The government ratified it on 31 October 2023, and it entered into force for the United Kingdom on 1 December 2023. No source checked in this review cites Recommendation Rec(2022)2 by name.

## The short answer

The United Kingdom already requires, and has required since long before 2022, the core of Annex 1 for larger sports grounds: a safety certificate, a local authority to issue and enforce it, and criminal offences for the main forms of football disorder, with banning orders available to a court.

It has not adopted the recommendation as a single national law or a single stewarding regulation. Safety, policing, and spectator service sit in different Acts, and in three legal systems. England and Wales, Scotland, and Northern Ireland do not share one stadium-safety statute. Where this review could read the position for England and Wales and not for the other two, that is stated.

Stewarding is the main gap relative to Annex 2. There is no national stewarding regulation of the kind the recommendation describes. Contract stewards who search or eject spectators need a Security Industry Authority licence. In-house stewards at a certified ground are exempt from that licence, and at grounds licensed by the Sports Grounds Safety Authority the certificate must now require equivalent training and vetting. Much of the rest of Annex 2, including a binding code of conduct, high-visibility numbers, and recorded exercises, was found only in guidance, or was not found.

## What has changed since 1 September 2022

These are the changes found. The safety-certificate regime itself was already in place.

- **1 December 2023.** The Saint-Denis Convention entered into force for the United Kingdom. That is the parent convention, not Recommendation Rec(2022)2.
- **April 2023.** The United Kingdom Football Policing Unit, which is the national football information point and which administers football banning orders for England and Wales, moved from the Home Office to the National Police Chiefs’ Council.
- **9 December 2022, to be in certificates by 30 June 2023.** The Sports Grounds Safety Authority directed certifying authorities at the grounds it licenses to write training and vetting conditions for in-house stewards into the general safety certificate.
- **1 August 2023.** The Authority published a set of stewarding factsheets. They describe a safety officer, stewards, and an integrated safety, security and service approach. They are guidance.
- **3 April 2025.** The Terrorism (Protection of Premises) Act 2025, known as Martyn’s Law, received Royal Assent. It will require qualifying premises, including many sports grounds, to have procedures for a terrorist attack. It was not yet in force at this review. Commencement was reported as expected in 2027.
- **2 August 2025.** The Safety of Sports Grounds (Designation) (Amendment) Order 2025 updated which grounds in England and Wales need a safety certificate. It did not change the certificate regime.
- **2025.** The Football Governance Act 2025 makes fan consultation a licence condition for clubs regulated by the Independent Football Regulator. The consultation covers operational and match-day issues, including ticket pricing. It is a governance duty for those English clubs, not a UK-wide safety duty.
- **22 March 2026.** The Unauthorised Entry to Football Matches Act 2026 came into force in England and Wales. Entering, or trying to enter, a designated match without a ticket the person is allowed to use is an offence. A football banning order can follow conviction. The Act also covers the use of a ticket or pass the person is not eligible to use.

A Scottish Government news page says ministers intended to bring a Bill to widen the behaviour that can lead to a football banning order in Scotland, including pyrotechnics, pitch invasions, and throwing missiles. This review did not confirm that Bill as an Act in force.

## Three systems, not one

**England and Wales.** A designated sports ground needs a safety certificate from the local authority under the Safety of Sports Grounds Act 1975. For Premier League and English Football League grounds the capacity threshold is 5,000. Otherwise it is 10,000. Covered stands holding more than 500 spectators at other grounds need a certificate under the Fire Safety and Safety of Places of Sport Act 1987. On top of that, the Sports Grounds Safety Authority licenses admission to designated football matches and oversees how local authorities certify those grounds. The Authority’s regulatory role is England and Wales. Outside that it advises.

**Scotland.** The 1975 Act applies in Scotland. It does not apply in Northern Ireland. Scottish Ministers exercise the designation functions. Local authorities issue certificates. Football banning orders are in the Police, Public Order and Criminal Justice (Scotland) Act 2006, not in the England and Wales Act. The Sports Grounds Safety Authority does not license Scottish grounds.

**Northern Ireland.** The Safety of Sports Grounds (Northern Ireland) Order 2006 is the certificate regime. District councils issue certificates, set a safe capacity, and must inspect designated grounds at least once every 12 months. Match-day offences and football banning orders are in the Justice Act (Northern Ireland) 2011.

## Topic by topic

Each topic is one cluster from the codebook. The word in bold is the judgement for the United Kingdom as a whole. Detail that differs by jurisdiction is in the paragraph.

### National governance and coordination — partial

The Sports Grounds Safety Authority oversees safety certification for designated football in England and Wales. The United Kingdom Football Policing Unit coordinates football policing and is the national football information point. No single body was found whose job is safety, security, and service together.

Local certificates are issued by the council. A certificate or a prohibition notice cannot require police officers unless the chief officer agrees the extent of that policing. That is a statutory split between the venue and the police. Safety advisory groups are the usual local meeting of the council, police, fire service, and club. They are established practice. A statute that requires a group of that name was not read.

A national strategy on safety, security, and service, kept under review, was not found. The convention ratification and the Authority’s description of the three pillars are the closest documents.

### Venue safety duties — met for the operator, partial for the rest

The club or ground management must take reasonably practicable steps to protect spectators, under the Health and Safety at Work etc. Act 1974. A failure that kills spectators can also fall under the Corporate Manslaughter and Corporate Homicide Act 2007. In Northern Ireland, Sport NI states that safety rests first with owners and operators, who must comply with the certificate.

A safety officer who plans and runs the event is described in the Authority’s stewarding factsheet of August 2023, which points to the Guide to Safety at Sports Grounds (the Green Guide). That is guidance. This review did not read a section of an Act that creates the post, nor a certificate that makes it a condition in Scotland or Northern Ireland.

Ground regulations are how clubs set the conditions of entry. Alcohol in sight of the pitch, and fireworks and flares, are banned at designated football by the Sporting Events (Control of Alcohol etc.) Act 1985. A statute that requires every ground to publish a set of regulations was not read.

### Safety certification and licensing — met

This is the strongest part of the framework, and it predates the recommendation.

A designated ground cannot admit spectators without a safety certificate. The local authority, or in Northern Ireland the district council, issues it and can amend, replace, or cancel it. The certificate exists to secure reasonable safety and, in Northern Ireland, the council’s published account of the Order says it states the safe capacity. Breach is a criminal offence. If the risk is serious, the authority can serve a prohibition notice and restrict or stop admission.

In England and Wales the Sports Grounds Safety Authority adds a licence to admit spectators to designated football matches, and it oversees the local certificates at those grounds.

The 2025 designation order only changed the list of grounds.

### Venue infrastructure and operations — partial

Certificates can require alterations to the ground. The detailed standards for seating, access routes, and barriers sit largely in the Green Guide, which certifying authorities use when they write conditions. This review did not re-read a statutory instrument that specifies those physical standards.

Separation of supporters and all-seated accommodation are long-standing features of designated football in England and Wales under the Sports Grounds Safety Authority’s licensing of those matches. The same point was not confirmed from a Scottish or Northern Ireland instrument in this pass.

CCTV is described in the stewarding factsheet as a typical safety system. A legal requirement for CCTV at designated grounds was not read.

The Authority has told a parliamentary committee that football venues have an operations manual covering the stewarding plan, evacuation, and medical provision. That was a description of practice and of guidance, not a section creating the duty.

### Emergency and contingency planning — partial

The Regulatory Reform (Fire Safety) Order 2005 requires a venue in England and Wales to assess fire risk and to record the preventive and protective measures. Certificate conditions deal with the wider spectator emergency. A single statutory duty to hold a named emergency plan, a named contingency plan, and recorded exercises was not read. Martyn’s Law will add terrorist-attack procedures for qualifying premises when it comes into force.

Whether certificates already require an annual recorded exercise is a question for a UK reader. It was not found in the sources checked.

### Venue safety service — partial

The factsheets describe the event-day structure: a senior executive, a safety officer, supervisors, and stewards. At grounds the Authority licenses, the safety certificate must require in-house stewards who search or eject spectators to be trained and vetted. That is a real duty, and it is new since 2022, but it is not a UK-wide statute that says every designated event must have a safety service.

No rule was found that defines when volunteers may be used in that service and what they may not do.

### Access, tickets, and removal from the ground — partial, and met for prohibited items

Prohibited items and behaviour at designated football are set in statute. The 1985 Act covers alcohol, fireworks, and flares. The Football (Offences) Act 1991 makes it an offence at a designated match to throw anything onto the playing surface or at a spectator, to go onto the playing surface, or to take part in racist chanting.

From 22 March 2026, in England and Wales, going in without an eligible ticket is itself an offence. Ticket touting under the older criminal law was not re-read in this pass. The 2026 Act does not extend to Scotland or Northern Ireland.

Searching spectators and refusing entry are security activities under the Private Security Industry Act 2001. Stewards may do them only inside that regime. A general power for any steward to search, and a stated consequence if someone refuses to leave, were not read as a single set of rules.

Access for disabled spectators is required in practice through the Equality Act 2010 duty to make reasonable adjustments, the Authority’s Accessible Stadia guidance, and Premier League rules that require a disability access officer. A stadium-specific access statute applying in all three jurisdictions was not read. The Premier League rule is reported by the Equality and Human Rights Commission. The rulebook itself was not opened.

### Policing, risk, and exclusion — met for banning orders and the information point

The United Kingdom Football Policing Unit coordinates national football-policing policy, keeps the overview of banning orders, and is the national football information point for the United Kingdom. The gov.uk page that states this has not been updated since the unit moved to the National Police Chiefs’ Council in April 2023. Policing is devolved in Scotland and Northern Ireland. A Home Office answer in October 2024 still described the unit, and Home Office funding for it.

Courts in England and Wales make football banning orders under the Football Spectators Act 1989. During a control period for a match abroad, the Football Banning Orders Authority can require the person to report to a police station and surrender their passport. The Crown Prosecution Service guidance says that authority keeps the central register of orders for England and Wales. Scotland and Northern Ireland have their own banning orders, under the 2006 Act and the Justice Act (Northern Ireland) 2011. Registers there were not read.

A legal scheme that sorts supporters into risk categories, with safeguards on how the category is used, was not found. Football policing uses intelligence and dedicated football officers. That is not the same as a statutory categorisation.

### Service, dialogue, and inclusion — partial

The Football Governance Act 2025 requires regulated clubs in England, as a condition of their operating licence, to consult fans on strategy, business priorities, operational and match-day issues including ticket pricing, heritage, and further fan engagement, and to take those views into account. That is new since 2022. It is not a supporters’ charter. The Act extends to England and Wales. Which clubs must hold a licence depends on the competitions it covers, and that definition was not re-read here. It is not the stadium-safety regime in Scotland or Northern Ireland.

Supporter liaison officers were met in an Equality and Human Rights Commission report on Premier League clubs. A legal requirement to appoint one at every club was not read. The same report says Premier League rules require a disability access officer.

Racist and indecent chanting at a designated match is an offence in England and Wales under the Football (Offences) Act 1991. Offensive chanting at regulated matches is an offence in Northern Ireland under the Justice Act (Northern Ireland) 2011. Scotland’s specific football offence in the Offensive Behaviour at Football and Threatening Communications (Scotland) Act 2012 was repealed in 2018. General criminal law still applies.

A legal duty to run fan embassies or fan projects was not found.

### Sports-event offences — met

Throwing objects, going on the pitch, racist chanting, alcohol and flare offences, and, since March 2026 in England and Wales, entry without an eligible ticket, are football-specific offences. Violence and disorder around a match are also general criminal offences, and they can trigger a banning order.

An attack on a player, referee, or steward is an assault under the ordinary criminal law. A sports-specific increase in the penalty when the victim is a steward or a member of the safety staff was not found.

### Steward status and authorisation — partial

The factsheets describe a steward as safety staff of the venue, in a chain of command under the safety officer, and distinct from the police. That description is guidance.

The binding regime is the Private Security Industry Act 2001. A person who searches spectators, refuses entry, or physically intervenes needs a Security Industry Authority door-supervisor licence, unless they are employed in-house at a ground that has a safety certificate. The exemption exists because those stewards were expected to be trained and vetted to an equivalent standard. In December 2022 the Sports Grounds Safety Authority told councils to put that training and vetting into the safety certificate for grounds it licenses, by 30 June 2023. In-house stewards doing that work must reach a Level 2 spectator-safety qualification within 12 months, and must not work unaccompanied on physical intervention until they have done the conflict-management and physical-intervention training.

A company supplying contract stewards for licensable work is inside the Security Industry Authority regime. There is no separate national licence, of the Annex 2 type, to run an in-house stewarding organisation.

### Steward duties and powers — partial

Crowd management, ticket checks, and entry and exit control are described as steward duties in the Authority’s factsheets and in the Green Guide. Search, detention for the police, and ejection are licensable security activities, so they are legally distinct from police powers. First aid and help to the emergency services are part of the guidance and of the qualification route. A statute that lists the steward’s duties in one place was not found.

Where police are on duty, the extent of policing is for the chief officer, not for the certificate. A required written agreement of roles, of the kind the recommendation calls a statement of intent, was not read.

### Conduct and visibility — not found as a legal requirement

A legally required stewards’ code of conduct, covering courtesy, neutrality, and a ban on discriminatory behaviour by the steward, was not found. Nor was a legal requirement that stewards wear high-visibility clothing with a unique number. Both are widespread practice and are the sort of detail the Green Guide and club instructions cover. They were not found as a binding rule in this pass.

### Records and supervision — partial

The December 2022 certificate conditions require training and vetting of in-house stewards at licensed grounds to be completed and kept up to date, which means the club must be able to show the council those records. A required safety handbook, issued to every steward and covering emergency and contingency plans, was not found as a legal duty. Briefing is covered in a factsheet.

### Training and exercises — partial

The binding training rule found is the one in the safety-certificate conditions for in-house stewards at Sports Grounds Safety Authority licensed grounds: Level 2 spectator safety, with conflict management and physical intervention before they intervene unaccompanied. The factsheets say qualifications and event-specific training are inconsistent across the sector, which is why the Authority has been researching a reform of stewarding.

A requirement that the training cover counter-terrorism and discriminatory behaviour, and a requirement that emergency plans be exercised and the exercise recorded, were not found in the instruments read. Martyn’s Law, once in force, will bear on terrorist-attack procedures at qualifying premises. It does not yet.

### Spectator service and private-security law — partial, and met for the private-security link

The factsheets treat “service” as the non-safety-critical role: making people feel welcome. They do not, in the pages read, impose a legal duty on stewards to support disabled spectators and to be the route for reporting discrimination. Premier League disability access officers are a league rule, reported by the Equality and Human Rights Commission.

The private-security link is met. The Private Security Industry Act 2001 is the national regime, and section 4(6) states how in-house stewards at a certified ground fit inside it.

## What a UK reader would be asked

These are the points this review could not close. They are the survey, pre-filled from the findings above.

1. Is there a published strategy on safety, security, and service, for the United Kingdom or for any of the three jurisdictions, and is it reviewed?
2. Is a safety officer a condition of the safety certificate in England and Wales, in Scotland, and in Northern Ireland?
3. Do Scottish and Northern Ireland certificates require the same Level 2 qualification and vetting for in-house stewards as the Sports Grounds Safety Authority directed in England and Wales in 2022?
4. Are volunteer stewards allowed in the safety role, and is their training defined?
5. Is CCTV a condition of the certificate at designated grounds?
6. Do certificates require a contingency plan and a recorded exercise, and how often?
7. Is there a written policy for categorising supporters by risk, and what safeguards does it contain?
8. Which competitions require a supporter liaison officer and a disability access officer, beyond the Premier League?
9. Is a stewards’ code of conduct, and high-visibility numbering, a certificate condition or only guidance?
10. Has the Scottish Government’s proposed widening of football banning orders been enacted?
11. Does any UK instrument cite Recommendation Rec(2022)2?
